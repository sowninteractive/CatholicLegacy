The Oldest Question in Christianity

Which Church did Jesus found — and is it still here?

Jesus never said he was founding an idea. He said he was founding a Church (Matt 16:18) — something visible, unified, teachable, and missionary. Everything you believe as a Christian was first taught inside that Church. The only honest way to find it is to ask what it looked like when it was young, and see which Church still looks like that today.

1

He founded a Church, not a book

“I will build my Church” (Matt 16:18). He gave it authority — “whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven” (Matt 18:18) — and promised to be with it “until the end of the age” (Matt 28:20). The New Testament is a gift of that Church, not a replacement for it.

2

That Church was visible and unified

In AD 107, St. Ignatius — taught by the Apostle John — wrote to churches across the Roman Empire: “Where the bishop is, there let the congregation be, just as where Jesus Christ is, there is the catholic Church.” You could point to it. You could walk into it.

3

That Church never disappeared

One creed, bishops in apostolic succession, the Eucharist, and unity with the successor of Peter — these marks were present in AD 100, AD 500, AD 1000, and AD 1500. Only one Church on earth still carries every one of them. It is the same one that met in the upper room.

In AD 451, when 630 bishops of the Christian world needed to settle what they believed about Jesus Christ, they did not debate — they stood and acclaimed the Pope’s letter aloud: “Peter has spoken through Leo.” That is what the earliest Christians sounded like.

For Our Protestant Brothers & Sisters

You already believe more than you think

If you are evangelical, Reformed, Anglican, or mainline, this is not a page that wants to mock your faith. It is a page that wants to start where you already stand. The first Christians held four things in common, and so do we — with you.

✓

One Holy Scripture

You defend the Bible as the inspired, inerrant Word of God. So do we. It was the Church, guided by bishops from St. Athanasius (AD 367) to the councils under Pope Damasus (AD 382), that recognized the canon and gave you your table of contents.

✓

One Savior

We agree with you that Jesus Christ is fully God and fully man, that his cross is fully sufficient, and that no one is justified by a merit of their own. As the Catechism teaches, his sacrifice “is unique, complete, and more than sufficient to reconcile the whole world.”

✓

One Grace

Catholics are justified by grace alone through faith. Say it slowly — the Council of Trent itself declared that no one can merit the initial grace of justification, and that even our good works are themselves gifts of grace (Session 6, Chapter 8–9).

✓

One Mission

The same Great Commission (Matt 28:19–20). The same call to holiness, the same love for the poor, the same urgent hope that your neighbors might come to know the Jesus you love.

So the question is not whether we love the same Lord. The question is whether Jesus intended a visible, enduring Church — and whether his first followers lived the way ours does. That is a historical question, and it can be answered with history. That is what the rest of this page is for.

The Chain of Successors

From the Tomb of Peter to today

A continuous, documented line of teaching authority — the longest unbroken institution in Western history. Break one link and the chain fails; it has never been broken.

  1. c. AD 33

    The Keys of the Kingdom

    Christ names Simon “Peter” (“rock”) and promises him the keys of the Kingdom (Matt 16:18–19). At Pentecost the Church is born, and the Apostles begin their mission to all nations.

  2. c. AD 42

    Peter in Rome

    St. Peter becomes Bishop of Rome, the center of the ancient world. He writes his two epistles from the imperial capital and seals his ministry with martyrdom under Nero in AD 67.

  3. AD 96

    St. Clement I, 4th Pope

    Writing to the Church of Corinth while Apostles still live, Clement intervenes in a disputed election — the earliest extra-biblical exercise of Roman authority, cited alongside Scripture by St. Irenaeus.

  4. AD 107

    “The Catholic Church”

    St. Ignatius of Antioch, on the way to his martyrdom in Rome, names the local churches “catholic” and writes: “Where Jesus Christ is, there is the catholic Church.” (Smyrnaeans 8,2)

  5. c. AD 180

    The Apostolic Catalogue

    St. Irenaeus publishes the definitive list of the Bishops of Rome — Peter to Eleutherius — arguing that Rome’s apostolic succession is the visible criterion of true faith against the Gnostic sects.

  6. AD 325 & 382

    Nicaea and the Canon

    The first ecumenical council defines the divinity of Christ and gives the Church the Nicene Creed. Sixty years later, a council under Pope Damasus confirms the canon of Scripture — the books you read today.

  7. AD 1517

    The Church Survives the Divide

    The Reformation splits Western Christendom, but not the chain: bishops keep ordaining bishops, the creed keeps being professed, and the successor of Peter continues to teach from the same chair in Rome.

  8. Today

    Leo XIV, 267th Successor

    From an upper room in Jerusalem to the Vatican City, the line has never been interrupted: one faith, one sacramental life, one pastor — exactly as it was in the first century, and exactly as Christ promised.

The Cloud of Witnesses

Wisdom of the Saints

The saints are the Church’s most persuasive argument — sanctity in every age, culture, and station of life, bearing fruit in the same faith. Many of them would be quoted happily in any Protestant church.

St. Augustine of Hippo

354 – 430 · Doctor of the Church

North Africa’s greatest mind — and the theologian Martin Luther and John Calvin both leaned on most heavily. After sixteen years of restless searching, he was converted by his mother’s tears and a child’s simple song.

“You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.”

Confessions, I, 1

St. Thérèse of Lisieux

1873 – 1897 · Little Flower, Doctor of the Church

A cloistered Carmelite who died of tuberculosis at twenty-four. Her “Little Way” of spiritual childhood — doing small things with great love — has made her one of the most popular saints of modern times.

“My vocation is love! In the heart of the Church I will be love.”

The Story of a Soul

St. Irenaeus of Lyon

c. 130 – 202 · Martyr & Father of Orthodoxy

A disciple of St. Polycarp, who learned directly from the Apostle John. He destroyed the Gnostic heresies and traced the true faith through the succession of bishops — the blueprint of Christian historical theology.

“The glory of God is a human being fully alive.”

Against Heresies, IV.20.7

St. Thomas Aquinas

1225 – 1274 · The Angelic Doctor

The Dominican friar whose Summa Theologiae marries the faith of the Fathers with the disciplined reason of Aristotle. Still the standard of Catholic theology eight centuries on.

“Grant me, O Lord my God, a mind to know you, a heart to seek you, wisdom to find you, a life to please you, and perseverance to wait for you.”

Asked in Good Faith

Five questions Protestants actually ask

The best Protestant objections deserve real answers, not slogans. Here are five of the most common — and what Catholics actually believe.

Q Did the Church add to the Bible?

No — she recognized its canon. For three and a half centuries Christians debated which books belonged: Hebrews, Revelation, and others were disputed. It was the Church who settled the question, first by St. Athanasius’s list of the 27 New Testament books (AD 367) and then by the council under Pope Damasus (AD 382). The authority that gave you your table of contents is the same authority that wrote it. As St. Paul himself wrote: “the church of the living God, the pillar and foundation of the truth” (1 Tim 3:15).

Q Where is the Pope in the Bible?

In Matthew 16:18–19 Jesus renames Simon “Rock” and promises him the keys of the Kingdom — the exact image of the royal steward in Isaiah 22:22, who receives “the key of the house of David.” In Luke 22:32 Jesus prays specifically that Peter’s faith not fail “so that you may strengthen your brothers.” In John 21:15–17 he commands him three times: feed my sheep. That is a job description, and Peter’s successors have held it ever since.

Q Do Catholics worship Mary and the saints?

Never. Worship is given to God alone. The honor Catholics show the saints is the honor you show a godly friend you admire — and the belief behind it is simple: those who die in Christ are alive and still praying. If you have ever asked a friend on earth to pray for you, you already believe what Catholics believe. Mary’s song, the Magnificat, was the first Christian hymn (Luke 1:46–55).

Q Are Catholics trying to earn salvation?

No. “By grace you have been saved through faith … not by works” (Eph 2:8–9) is confessed by Catholics at every Mass. The Council of Trent explicitly taught that no one can merit the initial grace of justification, and that even our good works are gifts of grace. Good works are the fruit of new life, never the payment for it — which is precisely what James 2 teaches.

Q Why does the Church matter — can’t I just love Jesus?

You can, and we want you to. But Jesus did not found an idea; he founded a visible people with pastors, sacraments, and a mission, and he promised to be with them “until the end of the age” (Matt 28:20). In John 17:20–23 he prayed that his followers be “one, so that the world may believe” — visible unity is not a human invention, it is Christ’s plan for reaching the world. The Catholic claim is simply that he meant what he prayed for.

“Where Peter is, there is the Church”

You do not have to decide today

— St. Ambrose of Milan, c. AD 385. If anything here has stirred something in you, that is not an accident, and you do not need to resolve it tonight. Ask the question honestly, read the primary sources, and talk to a Catholic you trust. Honest questions are the kind Christ answers best.
